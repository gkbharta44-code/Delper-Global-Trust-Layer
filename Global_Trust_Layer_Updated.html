Delper Global Trust Infrastructure: Executive Summary and Investment Plan

11-year outlook with turnover capped at 26 turns, GMV capped at 70% of world GDP, all after-tax profit reinvested until the cap, and a $4.0M investment for 20% of the company

Revised draft. Track A reinvests 100% of after-tax profit until GMV reaches 70% of base world GDP, which happens in Year 10. The seed investors exit in Year 1 through a buyback paid by GK Bharta. Capital, revenue, and the Year 10 cap are recalculated on that basis. GMV in every year is capped at 70% of base world GDP. 3. Revenue Streams

Stream 1: Software take rates

Delper removes a 5.1% share of the traditional friction cost, leaving a take rate of 24.2% on consumer and high-margin goods and 15.184% on premium commodities. Transactions must be at least USD 300.

Stream 2: Liquidity spread

Delper lends from its reserves on 14 to 90 day facilities at a stated 6.5% p.a. Partner banks take a 0.5% p.a. frontending fee, leaving Delper a 6.0% p.a. net spread secured by bonded cargo. On the revenue basis used here, this spread sits inside the 24.2% of turnover, so it is not added on top.

Stream 3: Sovereign BOOT concessions

Delper funds customs ports, logistics hubs, and data centers under 10-year build-own-operate-transfer contracts, and applies the 15.184% rate to port duties and clearing fees. BOOT activity begins in Year 6. BOOT revenue is not included in the revenue figures in this document; Section 12 shows only the GDP sensitivity.

Delper connects global buyers and vendors through one integration layer, holds payment in escrow, checks quality at an independent inspection stage, and sources orders through competitive bidding on price and logistics. Revenue comes from a software take rate, a liquidity spread, and, from Year 6, sovereign BOOT concessions (not included in the figures below). The seed investors exit in Year 1: GK Bharta pays the buyback of their principal and their 20% interest, and from then holds the company. Track A reinvests all after-tax profit until GMV reaches 70% of world GDP, which happens in Year 10 in this model. From that year, profit is paid to GK. The 70% cap compares transaction volume (GMV), which counts each step of a chain, with GDP, which counts only final output. It is a ceiling set by assumption, not a measured share of economic output.

10a. Recursive Turnover and End-Customer Test

Reinvested capital stays in the ecosystem and funds each 14-day cycle. Until the cap is reached, only OpEx and income tax leave the business. From Year 10, profit is paid to GK. This holds only if vendor payments and buyer collections settle within each cycle, so the full balance is free again at the next turn.

The end customer is the buyer, so turnover is recursive. The same buyer money is counted at each step of a chain. End-customer spend is therefore GMV divided by the chain depth, the number of steps a buyer's dollar passes through before it is consumed. Year 10 GMV of $119.45T is tested below against base world GDP of $170.64T.

13. Risks

  • Reinvestment dependence: Track A pays no profit to GK until Year 10. Growth depends on 26 turns a year and on settlement within each cycle.
  • Cap binding: From Year 10 GMV is held at 70% of world GDP, and effective turns fall as capital is frozen and GMV grows with GDP.
  • Capacity: 26 turns means a full 14-day cycle every year from Year 2, with no slack for delay, default, or disruption.
  • Year 1 capital: Year 1 turnover of $38.97M rests on $2.21M of trading capital. Set-up spend of $1.786M is not turned over.
  • Demand: Year 10 volume of $119.45T needs a market about 13 times the addressable pool the document sizes.
  • Customer pricing: Passing all other costs to customers raises the landed price. That weakens the best-price claim and may reduce volume.
  • Integration dependency: Disruption of SAP BTP Event Mesh or Proxtera endpoints would create transaction backlogs.
  • Regulatory exposure: Antitrust review by the US FTC or European Commission remains a risk. The antitrust immunity claim for BOOT concessions has no cited legal basis.
  • Banking treatment: Basel III ratios apply to a bank's own capital, so the treatment of reserves inside partner banks needs a regulatory opinion.
  • Licensing and settlement: Netting cannot remove settlement with parties outside the network, and licences for payment, lending, and custody in each market need confirming.
  • Income tax basis: Tax is 17% of pretax profit, with pretax taken as revenue less OpEx. The pretax base needs confirmation with a Singapore tax adviser.

14. Open Items Before Investment

  • Evidence that the plan can sustain 26 turns from Year 2, the full 14-day cycle.
  • Evidence that the market could supply GMV at 70% of world GDP by Year 10, and the basis for the GDP path.
  • Confirmation of the pretax base and the income tax treatment, with a Singapore tax adviser.
  • Treatment of the $1.0M liquidity reserve as CapEx or working capital, to confirm with an auditor.
  • Signed agreements or public confirmation for Proxtera, Tazapay, SGS, and each partner bank and government partner.
  • Escrow, bidding, logistics, and all other costs passed to customers, which are not yet costed in the investment plan. Confirm the principal or agent treatment with the accountant.
  • Legal basis for antitrust immunity and the regulatory treatment of reserves under Basel III.
  • Average chain depth for the commodity and e-commerce flows, which determines how much GMV becomes final consumption.
  • Evidence that vendor payments and buyer collections settle within each 14-day cycle.
  • Palm oil deal: evidence of USD 150.00M a year (10,000,000 metric tons at USD 15 per ton) to fund the Year 1 buyback.
  • Legal documents for the buyback of the seed investors' principal and 20% interest in each track.

15. Verdict

The plan is structurally coherent: a $4.0M CapEx programme is funded by the investment, Year 1 operating costs are met from revenue, and all after-tax profit is reinvested until GMV reaches 70% of world GDP. On that basis the plan reaches the cap in Year 10, after which profit is paid to GK. Holding 70% of world GDP as transaction volume is a strong demand assumption that the document does not evidence. Treat the figures as projections, and confirm the open items before any commitment.

16. Exit Paths

The seed investors exit in Year 1. GK Bharta pays the buyback of their USD 4.0M principal and 20% interest in Track A, as part of the USD 8.00M seed buyback. No external exit route is needed, so the modelled sale-value table in earlier drafts is removed. Any later route for a third-party investor would need a new valuation and agreed terms.
